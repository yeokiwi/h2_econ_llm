# Singapore-Cambridge A-Level H2 Economics (9570) – Tutor & Examiner

Built from the 2026 SEAB syllabus (9570_y26_sy.pdf) and the 2024 JC2 prelim papers and suggested answers from Temasek JC and Victoria JC (Paper 1 and Paper 2). Use this skill whenever the user wants to:

- explain an H2 Econs concept, diagram or analytical chain
- write a model answer to a case-study (CSQ) or essay question
- mark or give feedback on a student answer using level descriptors
- set new practice case studies or essay questions in the A-Level style
- plan revision or check whether a topic is in or out of the syllabus

Always write in Singapore/British spelling (e.g. labour, maximise, programme), and in the JC answer style shown below.

## 1. Exam at a glance (2026 syllabus)

| Paper | Format | Marks (weight) | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1: Case Studies | 2 compulsory case studies, 30 marks each. Each has 2-3 pages of text, tables or charts and 6-7 part-questions. About **12 marks are data response** and about **18 marks are higher-order** questions per case. | 60 (40%) | 2 h 30 min |
| Paper 2: Essays | 6 questions: Section A has 3 that are mainly micro, Section B has 3 that are mainly macro. Candidates answer **3**, with at least 1 from A and 1 from B. Each essay has **(a) 10 marks + (b) 15 marks**. | 75 (40%+20% = 60%) | 2 h 30 min |

- P1: about 40% of the marks test AO1+AO2+AO3 and about 60% test AO1–AO4 (evaluation).
- P2: about 40% of the marks test AO1+AO3 and about 60% test AO1–AO4.
- SEAB-approved calculators are allowed in both papers.

### Assessment Objectives

- **AO1 Knowledge & Understanding**: concepts, theories, principles.
- **AO2 Interpretation & Evaluation of Information**: read text, numbers and graphs, make valid inferences and note the limits of the data.
- **AO3 Application & Analysis**: apply theory to contemporary issues, perspectives and policies, and build coherent arguments.
- **AO4 Evaluation**: judge issues and policies critically, spot unstated assumptions, and synthesise the arguments into a well-reasoned judgement or decision.

### The decision-making approach (core of the syllabus)

Economic agents (consumers, producers, governments) make decisions under scarcity. They gather information, weigh benefits and costs at the margin, consider constraints and perspectives, and recognise intended and unintended consequences and trade-offs. They review their decisions as conditions change. Use this frame especially for "should", "decide" and "factors to consider" questions.

## 2. Syllabus map with scope limits

Stay inside the scope. Content marked **NOT required** below must not drive an answer, although it may appear in passing.

### Theme 1: The Central Economic Problem

- **1.1.1** Scarcity (limited resources vs unlimited wants) leads to choice, trade-offs and opportunity cost, seen from the view of consumers, producers and governments. The **PPC** shows scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, productive efficiency, full employment vs unemployment or under-utilisation (a point inside the PPC), and changes in productive capacity (a shift of the PPC).
- **1.1.2** Objectives of rational agents: consumers maximise utility, producers maximise profit, and governments maximise social welfare. Use the **marginalist principle**. The decision process is: information → benefits and costs → constraints → perspectives → intended and unintended consequences → review.
  - NOT required: cost-benefit analysis as a project-appraisal tool, or formal derivation of utility maximisation (awareness only).

### Theme 2: Markets

- **2.1.1** The price mechanism does three jobs: signalling, incentive and rationing.
- **2.1.2** Demand and supply. Market demand and supply are the sum of individual curves. A price change moves along a curve, while a non-price determinant shifts it. Covers equilibrium, shortage and surplus, and the effects on P, Q, consumer expenditure (= producer revenue), consumer surplus (CS) and producer surplus (PS). These effects depend on PED, PES, YED and XED. Apply this to real markets, including the **labour market**.
  - NOT required: Marginal Revenue Productivity (MRP) theory of labour.
- **2.1.3** Government intervention in markets: taxes, subsidies, maximum and minimum prices, and quotas. Analyse the effect on P, Q, expenditure, revenue, CS and PS, and how PED and PES change the outcome.
  - NOT required: tax or subsidy *incidence*.
- **2.2.1** Firms' objectives. Profit = TR − TC, and profit is maximised where **MR = MC with MC rising**. Alternatives are revenue maximisation, profit satisficing and market-share dominance. Firms may lack the information to profit-maximise precisely.
- **2.2.2** Short-run and long-run costs and revenues. Internal and external economies and diseconomies of scale shape the LRAC.
  - NOT required: deriving cost or revenue curves, or a detailed list of each type of EOS or DOS.
- **2.2.3** Firms' decisions and strategies:
  - Strategies include growth, diversification and shut-down; price competition; third-degree price discrimination; innovation and R&D; marketing; and collusion.
  - Firms take existing and potential competition into account.
  - Assess the impact on **efficiency** (allocative, productive, dynamic), **consumer welfare** (choice, quality, CS) and **other firms** (costs, revenue, profit).
  - Awareness only: the four market structures classified by number and size of firms, barriers to entry and nature of the product. Firms may exploit **cognitive biases** (sunk-cost fallacy, loss aversion, salience bias), face **technological disruption**, and weigh **social and environmental concerns**.
  - NOT required: diagrams comparing market structures, price-discrimination diagrams, shut-down-condition diagrams, or short-run to long-run equilibrium adjustment diagrams. The shut-down condition can still be explained in words.
- **2.3.1** The government's micro objectives are **efficiency and equity**.
  - The social optimum is where MSB = MSC. Deadweight loss (DWL) is the loss of net benefit to society when output is not at the social optimum.
  - An efficient outcome may still be inequitable. **Inequity is a distributional issue, NOT a market failure.**
- **2.3.2** Market failure is when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently. Causes:
  - public goods (non-rivalry, non-excludability, and awareness of non-rejectability)
  - positive and negative externalities
  - information failure, including asymmetric information leading to adverse selection and moral hazard
  - factor immobility
  - market dominance
  - Diagrams: use the two-diagram approach (MSC > MPC for negative externalities, MSB > MPB for positive ones). NOT required: diagrams for factor immobility, asymmetric information, moral hazard or adverse selection.
- **2.3.3** Micro policies: taxes and subsidies, quotas and tradeable permits, joint and direct provision, rules and regulations, and public education. Also covers **nudges** that use cognitive biases. Assess how effective each policy is, and the risk of **government failure**.

### Theme 3: The National and International Economy

- **3.1.1** Circular flow of income: households, firms, government and the foreign sector. National income = expenditure = output.
- **3.1.2** AD = C + I + G + (X − M) and the determinants of each component. AS and its determinants. Equilibrium real output and the general price level (GPL). **The multiplier**: know autonomous vs induced expenditure, the marginal propensities (MPC, MPS, MPT, MPM) and **k = 1/(1−MPC) = 1/MPW, where MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM**.
- **3.2.1** Standard of living (SOL) has material and non-material aspects.
  - Indicators: real GDP or GNI, real GDP or GNI per capita, unemployment rate, CPI (know base year and weights), balance of trade, **HDI**, and **Gini coefficient** (Lorenz curve, ranging from 0 to 1).
  - Know nominal vs real values and how to compare SOL over time and across countries.
  - Know the BOP accounts (current, capital, financial) at awareness level.
  - NOT required: calculating index numbers, national income, the current account, HDI or the Gini coefficient, or BOP sign conventions.
- **3.2.2** Macro issues, their causes and their consequences for SOL:
  - **Undesirable growth**: low or negative, unsustainable, or non-inclusive.
  - **Unemployment**: demand-deficient, structural or frictional.
  - **Price instability**: demand-pull inflation, cost-push inflation, or deflation.
  - **A persistently large BOT deficit or surplus**: caused by global conditions, competitiveness or exchange rates.
- **3.2.3** Macro policies:
  - **Discretionary fiscal policy** (G and T). Know about fiscal sustainability, and that transfer payments support inclusive growth.
  - **Monetary policy**: interest rates, and **exchange-rate management in Singapore's case**.
  - **Supply-side policies**: improve the quantity, quality and mobility of factors of production.
  - **Conflicts between objectives**. Know the **Marshall-Lerner condition** (|PEDx + PEDm| > 1), but not its derivation or calculation. Governments prioritise differently depending on the state of the economy and the country's stage of development.
- **3.3.1** Globalisation and trade:
  - Factors driving globalisation.
  - **Comparative advantage in terms of opportunity cost**. Numerical illustration is NOT required.
  - Benefits and costs of free trade and of capital and labour flows for consumers (prices, choice), producers (market size, competition, costs, innovation) and governments (macro objectives).
  - Protectionism, with a **tariff diagram required**.
  - Economic co-operation and trade agreements. NOT required: diagrams for trade agreements, or detailed institutional knowledge.

## 3. Decoding the question: Command / Content / Context

Every suggested answer in the prelims opens with a question-interpretation grid. Do this before writing any answer.

| | What to identify |
|---|---|
| **Command** | The command word and what it requires (see the table below). |
| **Content** | The concepts and theory needed, e.g. a "surge" in price calls for a demand shift plus PES < 1. |
| **Context** | The market, country or agent named, and the clues in the preamble or extracts that must be used. |

| Command word or phrase | What examiners expect |
|---|---|
| Compare | For 2 marks: 1 similarity + 1 difference (or overall trend + a notable exception), quoting the data. |
| Calculate (and compare) | Show the working and units, then make one valid comparison. Real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. |
| Explain / Explain how / Explain why / Account for | Give a rigorous causal chain with a diagram where relevant. No judgement is needed. |
| Using a (D&S / PPC / AD-AS) diagram, explain | The diagram earns a mark. It must be labelled and referred to in the text. |
| Explain two reasons or factors | Give two distinct, developed points, each linked to the extract. |
| Assess / Assess the extent to which | Analyse both sides and end with a reasoned judgement on the extent. |
| Discuss whether / Discuss the extent to which | Give thesis and anti-thesis (or policy 1 vs policy 2), then a judgement. |
| Discuss the case for and against | Weigh benefits vs costs of the decision, then take a stand. |
| Discuss the factors to consider | Give at least 2 factors, framed as benefits, costs, constraints or unintended consequences, then rank them. |
| "best", "most appropriate", "key solution" | You **must** compare the stated policy with at least one alternative. |
| "necessarily", "inevitably", "would cause difficulties" | Show when it does and when it does not, and give the conditions. |
| "combined effects" | Analyse each force separately, then weigh which one dominates using elasticity values or magnitude. |
| "different types" or "two countries" | Show how the outcome differs by type or country, e.g. by YED/XED values or by MPW. |

## 4. Paper 1 (case study) technique

### Typical data-response patterns (2–4 marks)

| Pattern | Mark allocation seen in the prelims |
|---|---|
| Compare two series from a figure or table (2m) | 1 similarity + 1 difference, with numbers or years. |
| Calculate and compare (2m) | 1 for the calculation, 1 for a valid comparison. E.g. Singapore 2022 real growth = 10.14 − 11.98 = −1.84%. |
| Explain why the PED value will change (2m) | The determinant (e.g. a larger share of income) + the direction (PED becomes more elastic). |
| Using a D&S diagram, account for a price change (4m) | 1 diagram + 1 shift factor from the extract + 1 elasticity explanation + 1 market adjustment process. |
| Explain why a firm can't expect higher profit (4m) | PED > 1, so TR falls (rectangle areas on the diagram), and with costs constant, profit falls. |
| Using a PPC, explain the effect on potential growth (2–4m) | The change in quantity or quality of a factor + its effect on productive capacity + an inward or outward shift, with a labelled diagram. A point inside the PPC means under-utilisation, not a shift. |
| Explain the opportunity cost (2m) | Definition ("the value of the next best alternative forgone") + the specific forgone option in context. |
| Explain why the market fails (4m) | Use the [P]–[ET]–[DQQ]–[D] framework (section 6.4). |
| Explain two reasons or causes (4m) | 2 marks each: identify the cause, then quote and analyse the extract. |
| Explain why a country specialises (2m) | Factor endowment from the extract + lower opportunity cost, giving comparative advantage → specialise and export. |
| Explain two factors MAS considers (4m) | E.g. persistence of inflation (appreciation lowers imported cost-push inflation) and growth prospects (appreciation hurts export competitiveness, assuming Marshall-Lerner holds). |

### Higher-order questions (8 and 10 marks)

The typical prelim mark scheme for an 8-mark question is **L2 4–6 / L1 1–3 + E 1–2**. For a 10-mark question it is **L2 4–7 / L1 1–3 + E up to 3**. Schools vary slightly, e.g. VJC allowed up to 3 E marks on an 8-marker.

- **L2** needs good scope and balance (both sides, e.g. how a policy works *and* its limitations, for two policies), good rigour (correct economic analysis with accurate diagrams), and good use of the case material.
- **L1** is one-sided or descriptive, lacks diagrams or analysis, or makes little use of the case.
- Cap: an answer covering only material *or* only non-material SOL was capped at L2 4m.
- **E** marks need a substantiated judgement: a stand plus at least one well-developed ATMS angle (section 7).

### Case-study rules

- Cite evidence precisely, e.g. "(Extract 3)" or "Table 1 shows…", and quote short phrases.
- Use the data to justify assumptions, e.g. "people need to eat" means PED < 1, and a 65% concentration ratio means oligopoly.
- Bring in your own knowledge only where it supports the case. The case context is what earns application credit.

## 5. Paper 2 (essay) technique

### Part (a), 10 marks: explain only, no evaluation

Mark scheme: **L3 8–10, L2 5–7, L1 1–4**.

- **L3** has good scope (all the parts the question asks for, e.g. the demand shift *and* PES), good rigour (D&S or AD/AS analysis, the market adjustment process or multiplier, a diagram) and good application to the preamble.
- **L2** is missing one of those elements.
- **L1** is descriptive, has conceptual errors, or has no context.

Structure:

1. **Intro**: define the key terms and outline the approach.
2. **KA1** and **KA2**: each opens with a topic sentence that answers the question, then gives the theory, a diagram, the chain of reasoning and the context.
3. A short wrap-up is optional.

### Part (b), 15 marks: analysis plus evaluation

Mark scheme: **L3 8–10, L2 5–7, L1 1–4**, plus evaluation marks of **E3 = 5** (well substantiated, a well-reasoned judgement), **E2 = 3–4** (some attempt at judgement, with possible logical flaws) and **E1 = 1–2** (an unexplained judgement).

Structure:

1. **Intro**: define the terms, set the context, and outline the approach or criteria.
2. **Thesis / Policy 1**: how it works, with a diagram, then its **limitations or unintended consequences**.
3. **Anti-thesis / Policy 2**: the same treatment, including its limitations.
4. **Evaluative conclusion**: a **stand** that answers the exact question, plus **two well-developed ATMS angles** (VJC standard; TJC accepted one).

For "factors to consider" questions, frame the factors as benefits (how it works), unintended consequences or costs, and constraints or limitations, e.g. information, funding or time lags. Then rank them.

### Essay-writing rules from the mark schemes

- Explain how P, Q, profit or GPL *change* and *why*. Do not just read values off the diagram.
- State ceteris paribus or assumptions explicitly, e.g. "assuming costs remain constant" or "assuming the Marshall-Lerner condition holds".
- When the question gives no context ("a country", "an economy"), supply realistic examples, e.g. Singapore vs the US, or a developing country.
- A numerical multiplier example is optional, but the round-by-round logic is required.

## 6. Analytical chains (the "rigour" toolkit)

### 6.1 Market adjustment process (use in every D&S explanation)

A factor changes willingness or ability (link it to MCOP for supply). The curve shifts. At the original price there is a shortage or surplus, which puts upward or downward pressure on price. As price changes, Qd and Qs move along their curves until a new equilibrium is reached at P* and Q*.

### 6.2 Elasticities

- **PED** depends on the number and closeness of substitutes, the share of income, the degree of necessity, the time period, and how broadly the good is defined.
  - If PED > 1, a price rise lowers TR. If PED < 1, a price rise raises TR.
  - A "sharp" price change usually means PED < 1 or PES < 1.
- **PES** depends on the time taken to produce, spare capacity, inventories and factor mobility. Hotels and housing have PES < 1 because of the time needed for licences and construction.
- **YED**: YED > 1 is a normal luxury (e.g. 5-star hotels), 0 < YED < 1 is a normal necessity (e.g. business hotels), and YED < 0 is an inferior good (e.g. capsule or budget hotels).
- **XED**: XED > 0 means substitutes and XED < 0 means complements. Its size shows the strength of the relationship, e.g. a strong vs weak complement determines how far demand shifts.
- **Consumer expenditure = producer revenue = P × Q.** With combined shifts, weigh the magnitudes using the elasticity values, and the effect may be indeterminate.

### 6.3 Government intervention (micro)

- **A specific tax or a mandatory contribution** (e.g. a green fund levy) raises MPC or MCOP. Supply falls, P rises and Q falls. When set equal to MEC at Qs, it internalises the externality and removes DWL.
- **A subsidy** lowers MCOP. Supply rises, P falls and Q rises. It strains the budget, has an opportunity cost, and may be fiscally unsustainable when PED < 1 because a large subsidy is needed.
- **A maximum price** set below equilibrium creates a persistent shortage (Qs to Qd). It needs non-price rationing (queues, priority access) and causes welfare loss and lower Qs.
- **A minimum price** set above equilibrium creates a persistent surplus.
- **A quota** limits Q directly and is useful for site-specific problems such as overtourism.
- **Tradeable permits, regulation, public education, nudges and direct provision**: always pair each with a limitation, such as imperfect information about MEC/MEB (leading to government failure), administrative and monitoring costs, time lags, or the opportunity cost of public funds.

### 6.4 Market failure framework for 4-mark "why the market fails" questions

1. **[P] Private decision**: producers or consumers weigh only MPB and MPC. MPB = MR for a firm, and MPC = the firm's MC.
2. **[ET] External effect**: a third party not involved in the transaction bears a cost or gets a benefit, using an example from the extract (e.g. dye chemicals polluting water and causing medical costs).
3. **[DQQ] Divergence and quantities**: MEC drives a wedge between MSC and MPC (assume MPB = MSB). The market produces Qp where MPB = MPC, but the social optimum is Qs where MSB = MSC, so there is over-production (Qp > Qs). Positive externalities are the mirror image, with under-consumption or under-production.
4. **[D] Deadweight loss**: between Qs and Qp, social cost exceeds social benefit, so there is DWL and allocative inefficiency, and the market fails.

**Public goods**: because they are non-excludable, free riders face no incentive to pay. The market has no incentive to provide, so there is non-provision, i.e. a missing market. Because they are non-rival, the marginal cost of an extra user is zero.

**R&D and patents**: R&D generates MEB, so there is under-production. Patents raise the private return on R&D, but they create a barrier to entry, leading to market dominance and allocative inefficiency. This is the trade-off to weigh.

### 6.5 Firms

- **Profit maximisation**: MC = MR with MC rising. Supernormal profit is (P − AC) × Q. High barriers to entry let firms keep supernormal profit in the long run.
- **Shut-down condition** (explain in words, no diagram needed):
  - In the short run, continue while P (AR) ≥ AVC.
  - In the long run, exit if P < AC, i.e. subnormal profits that persist.
  - E.g. a boutique gym closes when rising rent pushes up AC, changing fads and competition push down demand, and it makes persistent subnormal profit.
- **Higher costs** (MC and AC up, e.g. sustainable inputs or rising fuel) mean the new MC = MR gives lower Q, higher P and lower profit.
- **Demand-raising strategies** (marketing, product differentiation, green branding) shift AR and MR to the right and make demand less elastic, raising profit if revenue gains outweigh costs. Greenwashing is a context angle: firms may raise demand without bearing the costs.
- **Greater competition** (entry, deregulation, removing exclusive leases) shifts incumbents' AR and MR left and makes demand more elastic. P and supernormal profit fall, CS rises, and DWL and inequity fall. The counter-arguments are that firms lose internal EOS and so face higher unit cost, and that lower profit means less ability to fund R&D (dynamic efficiency).
- **Factors firms weigh when choosing a strategy**: funding ability, market structure and the level of competition (existing and potential), consumer preferences, cost structure, information, risk, regulation, and technology disruption.
- **Efficiency**: allocative (P = MC, or MSB = MSC), productive (producing at minimum AC on the LRAC), and dynamic (innovation over time).

### 6.6 Macro: AD/AS and the multiplier

- **Determinants of AD components**:
  - C: income, wealth, interest rates, expectations, taxes.
  - I: interest rates, business expectations, technology, corporate tax.
  - G: fiscal stance.
  - X − M: foreign incomes, relative inflation, exchange rates.
- **AS** depends on unit costs (wages, imported inputs, oil), productivity, and the quantity and quality of factors. Diagrams use a GPL vs real GDP (or real national output) axis. JCs typically draw a Keynesian-shaped AS with full-employment output Yf. Show:
  - a downward shift of AS when unit costs fall
  - a rightward shift of AS when productive capacity rises
- **Multiplier chain**: an injection (e.g. ΔG) raises AD. Workers get income and spend MPC × ΔY (induced C), which becomes someone else's income, and so on in successive rounds. Firms see unplanned falls in inventory and hire more labour. The process stops when the cumulative rise in withdrawals (S + T + M) equals the initial injection. **ΔY = k × ΔG, where k = 1/MPW.**
  - **Singapore has a small k**: a high MPM because it imports most raw materials and food, and a high MPS because of CPF. Large, less open economies like the US have a larger k.
  - The **reverse multiplier** applies to falls in X, I or G.
- **Unemployment**:
  - Demand-deficient: AD falls, real GDP falls, and firms cut derived demand for labour.
  - Structural: technology or restructuring creates skills mismatch and occupational immobility.
  - Frictional: workers moving between jobs.
- **Inflation**:
  - Demand-pull: AD rises near full capacity, e.g. a global rebound raises X.
  - Cost-push: AS falls because unit costs rise, e.g. supply-chain disruption or oil prices.
  - Deflation: falling GPL. Consequences include loss of purchasing power, redistribution, uncertainty, and export price competitiveness.
- **Growth**:
  - Actual growth is a rise in real GDP when there is spare capacity, shown by an AD increase or a movement towards the PPC.
  - Potential growth is a rise in productive capacity, shown by AS shifting right or the PPC shifting outward.
  - Sustainable growth means not degrading the environment or resources, and not causing inflation.
  - Inclusive growth means gains are shared, measured by the Gini coefficient. Transfers and retraining support it.

### 6.7 Macro policies

- **Fiscal policy**:
  - Raising G or cutting T raises AD, leading to a multiplied rise in real GDP.
  - Limitations: small k in open economies, time lags, crowding out or budget constraints, fiscal sustainability, and inflation risk near Yf.
  - Strengths in Singapore: large reserves and a prudent, balanced budget.
  - Transfers (Workfare, rebates) target inclusive growth.
- **Singapore's exchange-rate policy** (MAS manages the S$NEER within a policy band; "tightening" means faster appreciation):
  - Appreciation makes X dearer in foreign currency and M cheaper in SGD. Assuming Marshall-Lerner holds, X − M falls, so AD falls with a multiplier, which reduces demand-pull inflation.
  - Cheaper imported inputs lower unit cost, so AS rises (shifts down), which reduces imported cost-push inflation.
  - Trade-off: if the fall in AD outweighs the AS gain, real GDP falls and demand-deficient unemployment rises.
  - Why exchange rates, not interest rates: Singapore is very open with a high import content, and with free capital flows the interest rate is largely set by world rates.
- **Interest-rate policy** (for other economies): higher interest rates mean lower C (credit) and lower I (cost of borrowing), so AD falls. Limitations: interest-inelastic C and I, time lags, and capital inflows.
- **Supply-side policies** (training, R&D, infrastructure, Smart Nation, SkillsFuture):
  - Better quality or quantity of factors means AS shifts right (LRAS), and higher productivity means lower unit cost, so AS shifts down. This gives non-inflationary and potential growth.
  - Limitations: long time lags, uncertain outcomes, workers' willingness to train, cost to the budget, and the risk of displacing workers and causing structural unemployment.
- **Conflicts between objectives**: growth vs inflation, inflation vs unemployment, growth vs BOT, and growth vs sustainability. The answer turns on the root cause of the problem, its severity (e.g. 50% inflation in Sri Lanka), and the country's development stage.

### 6.8 Standard of living and indicators

- **Real GDP per capita**: adjusts for inflation and population, and is a proxy for material SOL.
- Limitations when comparing across countries:
  - Use **GNI** rather than GDP where foreign factor income is large, e.g. Singapore with its heavy FDI.
  - Use **PPP-adjusted** figures to compare purchasing power across countries.
  - Real GDP per capita ignores distribution (Gini), non-marketed activity, leisure, the environment and non-material aspects such as life expectancy.
  - Use **HDI** as a more holistic alternative.
- **Comparing over time vs across space**: over time, use real values and index numbers. Across countries, use a common currency and PPP.

### 6.9 Trade and globalisation

- **Comparative advantage**: a country has a lower opportunity cost because of its factor endowment (e.g. Ukraine's cheap farmland and deep seaports), so it specialises and trades, raising world output and consumption.
- **Intra-industry trade** (exporting and importing similar goods) is explained by product differentiation, consumer preference for variety, and economies of scale.
- **Tariff diagram** (required): domestic D and S, with world price Pw rising to Pw + t.
  - Effects: lower imports, higher domestic Qs, lower CS, higher PS, tariff revenue, and two deadweight-loss triangles.
  - Removing a tariff reverses this: CS rises, while domestic producers lose PS.
- **FTAs** (e.g. RCEP) bring larger markets, EOS, FDI, competition and lower prices. The costs are trade diversion (vs trade creation), structural unemployment in declining industries, loss of tariff revenue, and greater vulnerability to external shocks.
- **Deglobalisation**: the gains are resilience and protection of strategic industries. For Singapore, small domestic demand and reliance on X and FDI make the costs dominate.

## 7. Evaluation (E marks): Stand + ATMS

Every higher-order conclusion should give a **[Stand]** that directly answers the question, e.g. "Overall, X is likely to be the best / more appropriate / not necessarily…". Then add **substantiated ATMS angles**, labelled in model answers:

- **[A] Alternative or Assumption**: does a better or complementary policy exist? Does a key assumption (ceteris paribus, Marshall-Lerner, rational agents, accurate information) actually hold?
- **[T] Time frame**: short run vs long run, time lags, and long-term sustainability.
- **[M] Magnitude**: which effect is larger, e.g. elasticity values, the size of the multiplier, or the share of exports in GDP.
- **[S] Situation or context**: the root cause of the problem, the state of the economy, the country's features (small and open, ageing population, reserves), the nature of the product, or the firm's market structure.

Other angles examiners reward include priorities and objectives (efficiency vs equity), the **root cause** (supply shock vs market power), feasibility (funding, information), and unintended consequences.

Good conclusions from the prelims, as patterns:

- "A green fund is more appropriate as it tackles the **root cause** and is **financially sustainable** (the Seamless scheme in Extract 3 shows it can work)."
- "Shein likely lacks the incentive. **Greenwashing** lets it raise demand without bearing real cost increases."
- "Greater competition is net beneficial **because food demand is price inelastic**. However, if high prices come from a **supply shock** rather than market power, intervention is less warranted."
- "Whether price-stability policies inevitably cause trade-offs **depends on the policy and on fiscal capacity**. Appreciation's trade-off is likely unavoidable (**magnitude**), while supply-side policy funded by **Singapore's reserves** and paired with **SkillsFuture** can avoid it."

## 8. Diagram conventions

Always label the axes, curves, equilibria, shifts (with arrows and subscripts 0/1/2) and the shaded areas you discuss. Refer to every diagram in the text, e.g. "as shown in Figure 1, P rises from P0 to P1".

| Diagram | Axes | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| PPC | Capital goods vs consumer goods | An inward or outward shift for capacity changes. A point inside the curve shows unemployment or inefficiency. |
| Demand and supply | Price vs Quantity (of X per period) | Shifts, shortage or surplus, revenue rectangles (P×Q), CS and PS areas. |
| Price ceiling or floor, tax or subsidy | Price vs Quantity | Pc below equilibrium with Qs and Qd marked. S shifts by the tax or subsidy. |
| Externality (two diagrams) | Benefit/Cost vs Quantity | MPC, MSC, MPB = MSB (or MSB > MPB). Mark Qp, Qs and shade the DWL. |
| Firm (imperfect competition) | Price/Cost/Revenue vs Output | AR = D, MR, MC, AC. Profit-maximising point at MC = MR, and the supernormal or subnormal profit area. |
| AD/AS | GPL vs Real GDP (real national output) | AD shifts, Keynesian AS with Yf, a downward or rightward AS shift for supply-side changes. |
| Lorenz curve | Cumulative % of population vs cumulative % of income | Line of equality. Gini = area A / (A + B). |
| Tariff | Price vs Quantity (domestic) | Pw and Pw + t, imports before and after, CS, PS, revenue and DWL areas. |

When output is text only, describe the diagram precisely (axes, curves, shifts, points, areas), or draw a compact labelled ASCII sketch. If an image can be produced (e.g. matplotlib), render a clean labelled chart.

## 9. Singapore context bank (use where relevant, and check that current figures are up to date)

- **Small open economy**: trade is several times GDP, it imports most food, energy and raw materials, so it has a high MPM, a small multiplier and a high import content in exports.
- **MAS exchange-rate-centred monetary policy** (S$NEER policy band). It tightened repeatedly from October 2021 to fight imported inflation. 2022 CPI inflation was about 6.1%, the highest since 2008.
- **CPF** gives a high savings rate and a high MPS.
- **Fiscal policy**: GST was raised from 7% to 8% (2023) and to 9% (2024) to fund healthcare and an ageing population, with an Assurance Package and U-Save utility rebates. Singapore has large reserves and a prudent, broadly balanced budget over each term of government.
- **Skills and inclusive growth**:
  - SkillsFuture, including the Budget 2024 SkillsFuture Level-Up Programme: an S$4,000 credit top-up for those aged 40 and above.
  - Workfare Income Supplement.
  - Progressive Wage Model.
  - Pre-school subsidies.
  - A progressive income tax system.
- **Healthcare**: an ageing population, subsidies, MediShield Life, and means-tested grants.
- **Productivity and technology**: Smart Nation, digitalisation, Industry Transformation Maps, and R&D grants.
- **Sustainability**: the carbon tax, the Green Plan 2030, and investment in carbon-removal technology.
- **Trade**: an extensive FTA network (RCEP, CPTPP, and others) and openness to FDI.
- **Market examples**: electricity market liberalisation (Open Electricity Market), with retailers exiting during the 2021 energy-price spike; COE and ERP for road congestion; HDB for direct provision.

## 10. Common errors that cost marks

- Confusing a movement along a curve with a shift, or a point inside the PPC with a shift of the PPC.
- Saying "price rises because demand rises" without the shortage and market adjustment process.
- Reading P and Q off a diagram without explaining the economic reason.
- Treating **inequity as a market failure**. It is a distributional issue.
- Omitting "assuming costs constant", "ceteris paribus" or "assuming Marshall-Lerner holds".
- Ignoring the case material. In P1, context marks come from the extracts.
- Writing a one-sided answer for an "assess" or "discuss" question, giving how a policy works without its limitations, or giving no alternative for "best" or "most appropriate".
- A conclusion that only summarises without a stand, or a stand with no reasoning (E1 at best).
- Using out-of-scope material as the main argument (MRP, incidence, market-structure comparison diagrams).
- Comparing SOL across countries with nominal GDP, without PPP, per-capita figures or non-material indicators.
- Using interest rates as Singapore's main monetary tool.

## 11. Workflows

### A. Writing a model answer

1. Build the Command / Content / Context grid (it can be shown to the student as a planning box).
2. Check the marks: data response (2–4), higher-order (8/10), essay (a) 10 or (b) 15. Match the depth to the marks. A 2-marker is two crisp sentences, and a 15-marker is about 900–1,200 words.
3. Write the answer using the structure in section 4 or 5 and the chains in section 6. Label the KAs and diagrams.
4. For higher-order questions, end with a Stand + ATMS conclusion (section 7).
5. Optionally append the mark scheme (level descriptors and indicative content).

### B. Marking a student script

Return:

- **Mark awarded**: the L-mark plus the E-mark, with the level band and a one-line justification for each. For data response, give a point-by-point allocation such as "[1] diagram, [0] MAP missing".
- **What earned credit**: specific lines.
- **What's missing or wrong**: scope, balance, rigour, context, conceptual errors, diagram issues, and evaluation quality.
- **How to reach the next band**: 2–4 concrete, actionable fixes. Optionally rewrite one weak paragraph as a model.

Be consistent with the descriptors in sections 4 and 5. Do not inflate marks. Uncertain or illegible parts should be flagged.

### C. Setting practice questions

- **Case study**: write a title and 2–3 pages' worth of data: 1–2 figures or tables plus 3–5 short extracts with plausible sources, focused on a contemporary issue spanning micro and macro where possible. Set 6–7 part-questions totalling 30 marks (about 12 data response and about 18 higher-order), typically ordered 2, 2/4, 4, 4, 8, 10. Each question should require use of the data.
- **Essay**: write a short real-world preamble, then (a) [10] Explain… and (b) [15] Discuss / Assess… Section A is micro and Section B is macro. Ensure (b) needs a judgement and is not answerable from (a) alone.
- Provide suggested answers and mark schemes on request. Vary the themes across the syllabus and check the scope limits.

### D. Explaining concepts and revision

Give the definition, then the intuition, then the analytical chain, then a diagram, then a Singapore or real example, then a typical exam question on the concept and common pitfalls. Keep it exam-oriented.
